**In one line:** A short, well-defined list of NT passages carries genuine, significant textual uncertainty — knowing exactly which ones (and why) prevents both overclaiming ("the text is unreliable") and underclaiming ("everything is settled").
**What it is, per passage** (textual confidence lines from UBS5/Metzger [T-02][T-11]):
- **Mark 16:9-20** (longer ending): Absent from earliest Alexandrian witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus); NA28/UBS5 print it in double brackets [[ ]], signaling the editors judge it a later addition while retaining it as historically significant. `Majority view` among critics: not original to Mark; Byzantine-priority scholars (Robinson) and some evangelicals defend authenticity as `minority view`.
- **John 7:53-8:11** (woman caught in adultery): Also double-bracketed; absent from earliest manuscripts and found in inconsistent locations in others (some place it after Luke 21 or at John 7:36). `Majority view`: later insertion, floating tradition possibly historically old; textual confidence: NA28 double-bracket = "known but judged not original."
- **1 John 5:7-8 (Comma Johanneum)**: Trinitarian formula absent from all Greek manuscripts before the 14th-16th century; entered the Latin Vulgate tradition earlier. UBS5 does not print it at all (not even bracketed) — textual confidence: `consensus` among academic critics that it is a later Latin theological gloss with no early Greek support; historically defended by some in the King James/Textus Receptus tradition as `minority view`, largely on doctrinal rather than manuscript grounds.
- **Luke 22:43-44** (angel and bloody sweat in Gethsemane): Double-bracketed in NA28; early and widely attested in both directions (present in some early witnesses, absent in others of similar age) — `contested` among critics with real division, not merely `minority view`.
- **Matthew 6:13 doxology** ("For thine is the kingdom..."): Absent from earliest manuscripts; UBS3 rated its omission {A}, i.e. `consensus` that the doxology is a later liturgical addition (paralleling early Christian liturgical use, e.g. the Didache).
- **Romans 5:1** (ἔχομεν "we have" vs. ἔχωμεν "let us have"): A one-letter (omicron/omega) difference; UBS gives the indicative ἔχομεν a {C} rating — `contested`, genuinely difficult, since ancient omicron/omega pronunciation had merged, so scribes could have gone either way regardless of intended meaning; internal evidence (Paul's argument reads more naturally as indicative) favors ἔχομεν without full certainty.
**Why it matters for reading the NT:** This is the concrete, bounded list an informed reader should actually know — the rest of the NT text is comparatively far more secure than popular discussion (see [[tc-ehrman-misquoting-jesus]]) sometimes implies.
**State of scholarship:** See per-passage labels above; none of these is `speculative` in the sense of being unstudied — all have extensive T1 literature (Metzger's Commentary entry for each is the starting citation).
**Where it breaks / cautions:** Don't extrapolate from these six cases to "the whole NT is this uncertain" — UBS5 flags only ~1,400 variation units total as translation-significant, the large majority rated {A} or {B}; these six are deliberately the *hard* cases.
