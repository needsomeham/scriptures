**In one line:** NT authorship and dating range from near-**consensus** (the undisputed Pauline letters, Markan priority) to genuinely open **contested** questions (the Pastorals, 2 Peter, John's authorship) — knowing which is which prevents overconfidence in either direction.
**What it is:** Historical-critical scholarship dates and attributes NT documents using internal evidence (vocabulary, theology, historical references), external attestation (patristic citation), and comparative method (e.g. the Synoptic Problem's literary-dependence analysis). Results cluster into three rough bands: (1) essentially settled (the seven "undisputed" Pauline letters — Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon; Markan priority in the Synoptic Problem); (2) majority-but-contested (deutero-Pauline status of Ephesians/Colossians/2 Thessalonians, pseudonymous Pastorals, Markan community setting); (3) genuinely wide open (Q's existence, Johannine authorship/composition history, precise dates for Matthew/Luke/Acts/Revelation).
**Why it matters for reading the NT:** Whether a letter is judged genuinely Pauline vs. written later by a disciple in Paul's name changes how you weigh its theology against undisputed Paul (e.g. Ephesians' more "cosmic" Christology, or the Pastorals' church-order concerns) — and whether you read differences as development, situational adaptation, or a different author's hand is a live methodological choice, not a settled fact to memorize.
**State of scholarship:** See the claim-ladder examples above; as a general rule this vault treats "**majority view** among historical-critical scholars" (e.g. pseudonymous Pastorals) as distinct from "**consensus**" (e.g. Pauline authorship of Romans) and always names the dissenting position — typically evangelical/conservative scholarship defending traditional attribution (e.g. Wright & Bird, Carson & Moo's *An Introduction to the New Testament*) against the critical-majority position (Ehrman, Brown, most SBL-affiliated scholarship).
**Where it breaks / cautions:** "Most scholars think X" is not itself a claim-ladder label — always specify whether X is consensus, majority, or merely the loudest position in a given theological tradition's literature; check whether a cited source's confessional commitments predict its dating conclusion before treating the conclusion as independent evidence.
