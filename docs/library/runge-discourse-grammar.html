Discourse Grammar of the Greek New Testament (Runge)

Source
SourceT2intermediatepaid (~$35–45, Lexham/Hendrickson)Greek learning#discourse, #syntax, #intermediate

In one line: A discourse-linguistics account of why NT Greek authors chose one word order, connective, or construction over another — the "why this and not that" layer above basic syntax.

What it is: Steven E. Runge (PhD Stellenbosch; scholar-in-residence, Faithlife/Logos) applies functional/discourse linguistics (drawing on cross-linguistic typology, not just classical philology) to explain devices like historical present, left-dislocation, and connectives (δέ vs. καί vs. οὖν) in terms of what they signal to a reader, not just how to translate them.

Use in the path: Stage 3 (Reading Fluency) — read once basic morphology and syntax are solid; it directly serves this learner's "understand in context" goal by explaining stylistic/structural choices verse-by-verse rather than adding more vocabulary or paradigms.

Strengths: Genuinely explains authorial choice, not just grammatical form; heavily NT-example-driven; widely regarded as the accessible entry point to discourse analysis for this field.

Weaknesses / cautions: Discourse-linguistic categories are a newer and still-developing subfield relative to traditional syntax — some category boundaries (e.g., precise conditions triggering historical present) remain under discussion among discourse analysts.

Claims to note: Discourse-functional analysis of Greek connectives/particles is a majority view approach among linguistically-trained NT scholars today, supplementing rather than replacing traditional (Wallace-style) syntactic categories, which remain the consensus teaching framework in most seminaries.
