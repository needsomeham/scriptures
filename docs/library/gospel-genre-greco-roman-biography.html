Gospel Genre as Greco-Roman Biography

Concept
ConceptNT background#genre, #literary-criticism, #greco-roman-context

In one line: The canonical Gospels most closely resemble ancient Greco-Roman bios (biography) — a genre with its own conventions a first-century reader would recognize, quite unlike a modern biography or a myth.

What it is: Ancient bios (as in Plutarch's Lives or Suetonius's Twelve Caesars) focused on a subject's character revealed through selective episodes, sayings, and especially their death, without the chronological completeness or claimed dispassionate objectivity of modern biography; authors freely arranged material topically, compressed timelines, and included speeches reconstructed to fit the occasion rather than verbatim transcripts.

Why it matters for reading the NT: Recognizing bios conventions explains features that look strange if you expect a modern biography or newspaper report: differing chronologies between the Gospels (e.g., the Temple-cleansing's placement), extended attention to the Passion relative to the rest of the narrative (consistent with ancient bios's interest in a subject's death as revealing character), and speeches that convey substance rather than stenographic accuracy — all standard ancient practice, not evidence of unreliability by ancient standards.

State of scholarship: That the Gospels belong to the ancient bios genre (rather than, e.g., pure myth, midrash, or a sui generis "Gospel" genre) is now majority view, largely following Richard Burridge's What Are the Gospels? (1992, 2nd ed. 2004), which reversed a mid-20th-century consensus (Rudolf Bultmann and the form critics) that the Gospels were generically unique products of oral-tradition folklore. A minority view still holds that Mark in particular innovates a new genre rather than simply adopting bios conventions.

Where it breaks / cautions: "Ancient biography" does not mean "ancient standards of factual accuracy match modern journalism," nor does it mean "therefore unreliable" — ancient bios had its own truth-conventions (character-revealing selection, not fabrication) distinct from both modern historiography and modern fiction; treat genre identification as a reading-convention question, separate from the historicity question.
