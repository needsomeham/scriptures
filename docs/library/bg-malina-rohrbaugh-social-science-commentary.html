Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptic Gospels (Malina & Rohrbaugh)

Source
SourceT2intermediatepaid (~$40)NT background#greco-roman-context, #honor-shame, #social-science-criticism, #contested

In one line: Verse-by-verse application of Mediterranean social-science models to the Synoptics — influential, but its methodology itself is a live scholarly debate.

What it is: Bruce J. Malina (Creighton University) and Richard L. Rohrbaugh (Lewis & Clark College), founding members of the "Context Group," produced this 2003 (2nd ed.) Fortress commentary applying cross-cultural anthropological models (honor-shame, collectivist "dyadic" personality, patronage, purity) to Matthew, Mark, and Luke passage by passage.

Use in the path: Consult after bg-desilva-honor-patronage-kinship-purity gives the general framework — use this commentary to see the model applied to a specific pericope you're reading in Greek, but read it critically rather than as settled fact.

Strengths: Systematic, forces attention to non-modern-Western assumptions embedded in the text (e.g. reading a healing story through purity/boundary categories rather than modern biomedicine).

Weaknesses / cautions: The Context Group's use of generalized circum-Mediterranean anthropological models (often drawn from 20th-century ethnography of Spain/Greece/North Africa) to reconstruct 1st-century Judea/Galilee is contested methodology — critics (e.g., Louise Lawrence, Zeba Crook, John H. Elliott's own later modifications) argue it risks anachronism and essentializing a very diverse ancient Mediterranean into one model. Use its readings as a hypothesis to test against a passage's specific Jewish/Greco-Roman context, not a default lens.

Claims to note: "Dyadic" (group-oriented rather than individualist) personality as the ancient Mediterranean norm — majority view among social-science critics, contested by scholars skeptical of the model's cross-cultural transfer.
