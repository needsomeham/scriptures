Greek Alphabet and Diacritics

Concept
ConceptGreek learning#alphabet, #pronunciation, #reference

In one line: The 24-letter Greek alphabet, its diacritics, and the handful of look-alike traps that catch English-speaking beginners.

What it is: Public-domain facts about the letterforms, breathing marks, accents, and punctuation used in printed Koine/NT Greek. Pronunciation columns follow the two live traditions described in pronunciation-erasmian-vs-restored-koine; grammar-book framing per mounce-basics-of-biblical-greek.

Breathing marks: every word starting with a vowel or ρ carries a smooth breathing (᾿, silent) or rough breathing (῾, adds /h/) over the first vowel (or second, in a diphthong).

Accents: three marks — acute (´), grave (`), circumflex (῀) — mark where ancient Greek carried a pitch change, not stress. Why a beginner can mostly ignore them: they matter for distinguishing a few otherwise-identical word pairs and for advanced prosody, but you can read and parse correctly without producing them accurately from the start; learn to recognize, not reproduce, at first.

Iota subscript: a small ι written under α, η, or ω (ᾳ ῃ ῳ) — a former diphthong now silent (Erasmian) or merged into the vowel (restored Koine); still affects parsing (it's part of some dative endings).

Diaeresis (¨): over ι or υ, marks that it starts a new syllable rather than forming a diphthong with the preceding vowel (e.g., Ἠσαΐας).

Punctuation: Greek uses a raised dot (·) for what English marks with a semicolon/colon, and a semicolon (;) as its question mark — the opposite of English convention; the comma and period look the same as in English.

Five common confusions for English readers:

  1. ν (nu) looks like Latin v — it's an "n" sound.
  2. ρ (rho) looks like Latin p — it's an "r" sound.
  3. η (eta) looks like Latin n — it's a vowel ("ē"/"i").
  4. ω (omega) looks like Latin w — it's an "o" sound.
  5. χ (chi) looks like Latin x — it's a "kh" sound, not "ks" (that's ξ).

State of scholarship: Pronunciation is contested pedagogically — which system to teach with — but not linguistically: which sounds the 1st-century Koine speaker actually used is a majority view among historical phonologists that restored Koine approximates it (per Buth and the Biblical Language Center tradition, building on W. Sidney Allen's reconstruction methods), while Erasmian is acknowledged by its own users (e.g., Wallace) as a pedagogical convention rather than a historical claim. See pronunciation-erasmian-vs-restored-koine for the full discussion and dissenting framing.
