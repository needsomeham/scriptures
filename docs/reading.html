What the Greek adds
5 notes · claim ladder marked
ὃ, not ὅςcontested (emphasis)
The relative is neuter — “that which” — four times in v.1, not masculine “he whom”: the object is a reality, a message, before it is a person — and yet it is “handled.” Brown (Anchor Bible) takes the neuter as deliberately broad, the whole Christ-event; Marshall (NICNT) reads through it to the person.
Three tense-forms, one sentencemajority view · perfect contested
ἦν (imperfect) → ἀκηκόαμεν, ἑωράκαμεν (perfect) → ἐθεασάμεθα, ἐψηλάφησαν (aorist). The majority view is that the choice encodes aspect — how the author frames the action — more than time. What “perfect” itself means is contested: Porter (stative), Fanning (blended), Campbell (proximity).
Main verb arrives latemajority view · discourse grammar
v.1's four ὃ-clauses are a fronted object with no verb; v.2 is a parenthesis (it does contain ἀπαγγέλλομεν); the sentence resolves only at v.3, ἀπαγγέλλομεν καὶ ὑμῖν. In discourse grammar such fronting is marked — the backlog you carry is the point (Runge).
καί … καί … and κοινωνίαconsensus range · sense contested
v.2's repeated καί (polysyndeton) slows the line on purpose. κοινωνία is wider than “fellowship”: association, participation or sharing in, even material contribution (BDAG). The range is consensus; the dominant sense here is contested.
v.4: ἡμῶν or ὑμῶνtextual · rating not verified
“Our joy” or “your joy” — one letter. UBS5/NA28 print ἡμῶν; the letter rating is not verified here (a lay apparatus reports {B}). In later Koine η and υ converged in sound, so the two were homophones: a scribe copying by ear could not tell them apart.
